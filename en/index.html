Reference

Investment Glossary

A collection of basic stock investing terms and formulas, explained simply for beginner investors. Click each term to expand its explanation.

Beta (β)

Measures a stock's volatility or sensitivity relative to the overall market (e.g. IHSG). A beta of 1 means the stock moves in line with the market; above 1 means more volatile, below 1 means more stable. Used as an input in the DCF & DDM calculators on this page.

Cost of Equity

The return investors expect for bearing the risk of holding a stock. Usually calculated with the CAPM (Capital Asset Pricing Model), combining the risk-free rate, beta, and expected market return.

Cost of Equity = Risk Free + Beta × (Market Return − Risk Free)
Current Ratio

The ratio of current assets to current liabilities, indicating a company's short-term liquidity — how well it can cover obligations due soon.

Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities
Debt to Equity Ratio (DER)

The ratio of total debt to shareholders' equity. Shows how aggressively a company's operations are funded by debt versus its own capital. A very high DER signals greater financial risk.

DER = Total Debt ÷ Total Equity
Dividend Payout Ratio

The percentage of net income paid out as dividends to shareholders. The remainder is retained by the company for expansion or operations.

Payout Ratio = Total Dividends ÷ Net Income
Dividend Per Share (DPS)

The amount of cash dividend a company pays out for each share outstanding. The main input for the Dividend Discount Model (DDM) calculator on this page.

Dividend Yield

Dividend per share as a percentage of the current stock price — shows the cash "return" an investor gets from dividends alone, separate from potential price appreciation.

Dividend Yield = DPS ÷ Stock Price
Earning Per Share (EPS)

A company's net income allocated to each outstanding share. One of the most basic fundamental figures, used in the Equity Bond Valuation calculator on this page.

EPS = Net Income ÷ Shares Outstanding
Enterprise Value (EV)

The total value of a company from a full-acquisition standpoint — equity value (market cap) plus total debt, minus cash and cash equivalents.

EV = Market Cap + Total Debt − Cash & Equivalents
EV/EBITDA

A ratio comparing Enterprise Value to EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization). Useful for comparing companies across sectors or capital structures, since it's unaffected by how a company is financed (debt vs. equity).

Intrinsic Value

An estimate of a stock's fair value based on company fundamentals (projected earnings, dividends, or cash flow) — not the price shaped by momentary market sentiment. This is what every calculator on this page computes.

Margin of Safety

The gap between intrinsic value and the current market price — a "cushion" value investors look for before buying, to account for estimation errors or unexpected market conditions.

Margin of Safety = (Intrinsic Value − Market Price) ÷ Intrinsic Value
Market Capitalization (Market Cap)

The total market value of a company's outstanding shares — the most common way to measure a company's "size" on the exchange.

Market Cap = Stock Price × Shares Outstanding
Net Profit Margin (NPM)

Net income as a percentage of total revenue. Shows how efficiently a company converts sales into actual profit.

NPM = Net Income ÷ Revenue
Price to Book Value (PBV / P/B)

The ratio of stock price to book value (net assets) per share. Often used to value banking and financial sector stocks, where asset value is more relevant than earnings projections.

PBV = Stock Price ÷ Book Value per Share
Price to Earning Ratio (PER / P/E)

The ratio of stock price to net income per share (EPS). Shows how many times investors are willing to pay relative to the company's annual earnings. A low P/E can mean cheap (or a fundamental problem), a high P/E can mean expensive (or high growth expectations).

PER = Stock Price ÷ EPS
Return on Assets (ROA)

Measures how efficiently a company generates profit from all the assets it owns, regardless of whether those assets are funded by debt or equity.

ROA = Net Income ÷ Total Assets
Return on Equity (ROE)

Measures how efficiently a company generates profit from shareholders' invested capital. One of the most important profitability indicators in value investing.

ROE = Net Income ÷ Total Equity
Return on Investment (ROI)

Measures the profit an investor earns relative to the capital invested — can be used to measure personal investment results, not just company performance.

ROI = (Ending Value − Initial Capital) ÷ Initial Capital
Terminal Value

The projected value of a company's earnings/cash flow after the explicit projection period ends (e.g. after year 10), calculated assuming constant growth forever (perpetuity). A key component in the DCF and DDM calculators on this page.

Frequently Asked Questions About Stock Valuation

What is a stock's intrinsic value?

Intrinsic value is an estimate of a stock's fair value based on projected future earnings, dividends, or cash flows — not a price shaped by market sentiment. This calculator computes it using 4 methods: DCF, DDM, Equity Bond Valuation, and a Financial Freedom simulator.

What's the difference between DCF and DDM in stock valuation?

DCF discounts a company's projected net earnings, while DDM discounts projected dividends per share. DDM is more accurate for companies that pay dividends regularly, while DCF is more flexible even if a company doesn't pay dividends yet.

What is Warren Buffett-style Equity Bond Valuation?

A method from the book "The Warren Buffett Stock Portfolio" that treats a stock like a bond — calculating the estimated total return (IRR) from the current price and comparing it to the 10-year government bond yield as a risk-free benchmark.

What's the difference between the DCF here and the DCF used by professional analysts?

The essence and formulas are exactly the same as those used by professional analysts (cash flow projections, discount rate, and terminal value). The difference is that this calculator is simplified and faster so retail investors can use it right away without building an Excel model from scratch — without changing the essence of the calculation.
